# Alleato Group — Superintendent / Field Leader Handbook

*Your partner from the ground up.*

**Purpose:** This handbook teaches the core field-side skills needed to run a jobsite — from setting up the site and planning the work to quality, safety, and leading the crews and trades. Each chapter is a practical "how we do it in the field" guide. Pair each chapter with the curated videos and courses in the Training Hub.

**How to use it:** New assistant supers and foremen should start with Chapters 1, 2, 4, and 5 (site setup, documentation, quality, safety) — these protect the job every single day. Superintendents should be fluent in all chapters. The office (PM) side of the same skills lives in the PM Handbook — read both; the best field leaders understand the paper, and the best PMs understand the field.

## 1. Site Logistics & Site Planning

**Why it matters:** How you set up the site determines how efficiently every trade works for the whole job. A good logistics plan prevents congestion, double-handling of material, safety hazards, and access fights.

**What a site logistics plan covers:**

- **Access & haul routes** — how trucks and workers get in and out; gates, one-way flow, flagging.
- **Laydown & staging areas** — where material lands and is stored, by phase (they change as the building goes up).
- **Crane / hoisting** — location, swing radius, pick zones, and lift planning.
- **Temporary facilities** — trailers, parking, toilets, dumpsters, temp power and water, wash stations.
- **Fencing, security, and signage.**
- **Fire lanes and emergency access** kept clear at all times.

**How to build it:**

1. Start from the **site/civil drawings** and the **schedule** — logistics change by phase (excavation → structure → skin → finishes).
2. Plan **material flow** to minimize double-handling: land material close to where it installs, when it installs.
3. Locate the **crane/hoist** to serve the most work with the fewest relocations; verify swing radius and overhead/utility clearances.
4. Coordinate **deliveries** with a delivery schedule so trades aren't fighting for the gate or the crane.
5. **Publish the plan** and update it as phases change; review it with every new sub at their kickoff.

**Common mistakes:** A day-one plan that's never updated as the building rises; laydown too far from the work; crane placed for convenience today but in the way next month; blocked fire lanes.

## 2. Daily Reports & Jobsite Documentation

**Why it matters:** The daily report is the legal and factual record of the job. When there's a delay claim, a dispute, or a warranty question a year later, the daily logs are what protect Alleato. "If it isn't documented, it didn't happen."

**What goes in a daily report:**

- **Weather** (and its impact on work)
- **Manpower** — each sub, headcount, and what they worked on
- **Equipment** on site
- **Work performed** and areas
- **Deliveries** received
- **Delays, disruptions, and issues** — and their cause
- **Safety** observations, incidents, and toolbox talks held
- **Visitors / inspections** (AHJ, testing agency, owner)
- **Photos** — dated, with location/context

**How to do it well:**

1. Fill it out **every day, the same day** — not from memory on Friday.
2. Be **specific and factual** — "Sub X had 4 men installing hangers on Level 2 east; delayed 2 hrs waiting on inspection" beats "worked on Level 2."
3. Document **delays and their cause** in real time — this is your evidence if a claim arises.
4. Take **consistent progress photos** and organize them by date/area.
5. Never editorialize or assign blame speculatively — stick to facts.

**Common mistakes:** Vague entries; skipping days; not photographing covered/buried work before it's concealed; failing to document delays when they happen.

**Alleato tip:** Keep daily reports in our project management software so reports, photos, and manpower live with the rest of the project record.

## 3. Field Scheduling — Look-Aheads, Pull Planning & the Last Planner System

**Why it matters:** The master CPM schedule (PM Handbook Ch. 9) tells you the plan; the field turns it into reality week by week. Short-interval planning and collaborative pull planning are how superintendents actually drive the work and keep trades flowing.

**Key tools:**

- **Look-ahead schedule (3–6 week)** — the detailed near-term plan, broken to the task/crew level, updated weekly.
- **Pull planning** — the trades plan a phase *backward* from a milestone, each handing off to the next, so the sequence is built by the people doing the work.
- **Last Planner System (LPS)** — the Lean framework: Master schedule → Phase (pull) planning → Look-ahead with **constraint removal** → **Weekly Work Plan** → measure **PPC (Percent Plan Complete)** and learn from misses.
- **Constraints** — anything that must be cleared before a task can start (info, material, prior work, inspection, equipment, manpower).

**How to run it:**

1. Hold a **pull planning session** for each major phase with all trade foremen — build the sequence together with sticky notes/cards, milestone backward.
2. Maintain a rolling **6-week look-ahead**; each week, identify and **remove constraints** on upcoming work.
3. Publish a **Weekly Work Plan** of only tasks that are truly ready (constraint-free) — these are commitments.
4. At week's end, measure **PPC** (tasks completed vs. planned) and discuss **why** any were missed — fix the root cause, don't just reschedule.
5. Keep the field look-ahead **tied to the master schedule** so slips surface early to the PM.

**Watch:** For a practical, step-by-step walk-through of running a pull plan, see the Last Planner / pull-planning video series linked in the Resource Library (Field · 03).

**Common mistakes:** Committing trades to work that still has open constraints; planning *to* the crews instead of *with* them; not measuring PPC so the same misses repeat.

## 4. Quality Control (QA/QC)

**Why it matters:** Catching quality issues before work is covered or the next trade builds on top of it is vastly cheaper than rework. Quality is a superintendent's daily responsibility, not an inspector's afterthought.

**The three-phase control system** (the industry/federal standard, worth using on any job):

1. **Preparatory phase** — *before* a trade starts a definable feature of work: review the spec and drawings, confirm submittals are approved, confirm materials on site are correct, review the plan and required inspections/tests.
2. **Initial phase** — at the *start* of the work: inspect the first installation, confirm it meets the standard, establish the level of workmanship, and correct issues before the trade proceeds.
3. **Follow-up phase** — *ongoing*: routine checks that the established standard is maintained through completion.

**Also core to QC:**

- **Pre-installation meetings** with the sub for critical/complex scopes.
- **Mock-ups** for finishes and exterior systems — the approved standard everyone builds to.
- **Inspection & Test Plans (ITPs)** — what gets inspected/tested, when, and by whom.
- **Documenting** inspections, tests, and any non-conformances to closure.

**How to run it:**

1. Build QC requirements from **Division 01 and each spec section** (PM Handbook Ch. 2).
2. Hold **preparatory and initial** meetings for every definable feature of work.
3. Verify **materials match approved submittals** before install.
4. **Inspect the first, then spot-check** — don't wait for the whole area to be done.
5. **Document everything**, including photos of work about to be concealed.

**Common mistakes:** Skipping the preparatory/initial phase and finding problems after the area is finished; no mock-up so "acceptable" is a matter of opinion; not photographing buried work.

## 5. Safety Management

**Why it matters:** Safety is the number-one responsibility on any jobsite — everyone goes home the same way they came in. It's also a legal obligation and a major driver of insurance cost and reputation.

**The essentials:**

- **OSHA training** — 10-hour for workers, **30-hour for supervisors** (supers/foremen should hold the OSHA 30).
- **JHA / JSA (Job Hazard/Safety Analysis)** — break each task into steps, identify hazards, define controls. Required for high-risk work.
- **Toolbox talks** — short, regular (typically daily/weekly) crew safety briefings on a relevant hazard.
- **The Focus Four hazards** — falls, struck-by, caught-in/between, electrocution — cause most construction fatalities. Fall protection is #1.
- **Site-specific safety plan, SDS binder, emergency action plan, and first-aid/incident procedures.**
- **Inspections & PPE enforcement** — daily walks, correct hazards immediately.

**How to run it:**

1. Establish the **site-specific safety plan** and orient every new worker before they start.
2. Require **JHAs** for the day's high-risk tasks and review them with the crews.
3. Hold **toolbox talks** consistently and document them in the daily report.
4. **Walk the site daily** for hazards — especially the Focus Four — and correct on the spot.
5. **Investigate every incident and near-miss**, document it, and fix the root cause.

**Common mistakes:** Treating safety as paperwork instead of behavior; letting "just this once" shortcuts slide; not documenting toolbox talks and inspections; weak fall-protection enforcement.

**Note:** Safety is a serious topic. Follow OSHA requirements and Alleato's safety program; when in doubt, stop the work and get help.

## 6. Layout & Surveying Basics

**Why it matters:** Everything is built off the layout. A layout error propagates into every trade — walls, MEP, embeds — and is expensive to fix once concealed. Supers must understand layout even when a survey crew performs it.

**Core concepts:**

- **Benchmarks & control points** — the known reference points (horizontal control and a vertical **datum/benchmark**) everything is measured from.
- **Grid lines** — the building's coordinate system on the drawings; layout locates real-world points to those grids.
- **Elevations / grade** — height relative to the datum (finish floor, top of steel, etc.).
- **Instruments** — builder's level/transit, **total station** (angles + distances to lay out points precisely), and increasingly robotic total stations and GPS.

**How it works in the field:**

1. Establish and **protect control points and the benchmark** — verify them before relying on them.
2. Lay out from **control, not from other trades' work** (which may be off).
3. Set up the **total station** over a known point, back-sight a second known point, then shoot/stake the layout points.
4. **Check elevations** against the datum for slabs, embeds, and openings.
5. **Verify critical layout** independently before major pours or installs.

**Common mistakes:** Building off someone else's (possibly wrong) work instead of control; a disturbed benchmark no one re-checked; not verifying layout before it's permanent.

## 7. Subcontractor Coordination & Managing Trades

**Why it matters:** A commercial job is mostly subcontracted. The superintendent's core job is orchestrating many trades so they don't collide — the difference between a smooth job and constant rework and delays.

**How to coordinate the trades:**

1. **Kick off each sub** with a start-up meeting: scope, schedule, logistics plan, safety expectations, quality standard, and coordination with adjacent trades.
2. **Sequence the work** so trades flow through areas cleanly (see pull planning, Ch. 3) — avoid stacking trades on top of each other in the same space.
3. Run a **weekly subcontractor coordination meeting** — review the look-ahead, confirm manpower commitments, surface conflicts, clear constraints.
4. **Coordinate MEP tightly** — overhead systems (duct, pipe, conduit, sprinkler, cable tray) fight for the same ceiling space; resolve routing before install (BIM coordination on complex jobs).
5. **Hold subs to commitments** — manpower, schedule, cleanup of their own debris, and quality — professionally but firmly. Document shortfalls in the daily report.

**Common mistakes:** Letting trades self-sequence into conflicts; no MEP coordination so systems clash in the field; not holding subs to manpower commitments; poor communication between shifts/areas.

## 8. Inspections & Working with the AHJ

**Why it matters:** The **Authority Having Jurisdiction** (building/fire/health inspectors) must approve the work at code-required milestones. Failed or missed inspections stop the job. A good relationship with the AHJ keeps work flowing.

**Know the rhythm:**

- Inspections happen at **defined milestones** — footings/rebar before pour, underground before backfill, rough-in (framing, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, fire) before cover, above-ceiling before close, and **final** before Certificate of Occupancy.
- **Special inspections** (by a third-party testing agency) are required for certain structural/fire items and are separate from the AHJ's inspections.

**How to manage it:**

1. Know **which inspections are required and when**, built into the look-ahead — never let a trade cover work that needs inspection first.
2. **Schedule inspections in advance** and make sure the work and the approved documents are ready and on site.
3. **Be present and prepared** — walk the inspector, know your work, have the stamped drawings and any required documentation.
4. **Build a professional relationship** with inspectors — respect, preparation, and honesty pay off for the whole job.
5. **Log every inspection** (pass/fail, corrections) in the daily report and correct any failures promptly, then re-inspect.

**Common mistakes:** Covering work before its inspection; calling for inspection when the work or paperwork isn't ready (fails cost credibility and time); not knowing special-inspection requirements.

**Alleato tip:** Fire-protection and ASRS scopes carry specific inspection and testing requirements (NFPA/FM) — coordinate these early with the AHJ and testing agency.

## 9. Reading Drawings in the Field

**Why it matters:** The super lives in the drawings all day — verifying installs, answering trade questions, spotting conflicts before they're built. (The full drawing-set breakdown is in the PM Handbook Ch. 1; this is the field-use view.)

**Field priorities when reading drawings:**

1. Always work from the **current revision** — confirm you're not building from a superseded sheet; track ASIs/bulletins.
2. **Coordinate disciplines** in the field the same way the office does — overlay architectural, structural, and MEP for the area you're about to build.
3. Follow **detail callouts** to the actual details before the trade installs.
4. Reconcile the **drawings against the specs** for the product/quality standard.
5. When the documents can't be built as drawn, **stop and RFI** (PM Handbook Ch. 4) — don't let a trade "figure it out" in a way that creates rework.

**Common mistakes:** Building from an old sheet; trusting one discipline without checking the others; letting a trade improvise around a conflict instead of getting it resolved in writing.

## 10. Managing Crews & Field Leadership

**Why it matters:** Superintendents lead people, not just work. Planning, communication, and leadership are what turn a schedule into finished building. NCCER and AGC both build formal curricula around exactly these skills.

**The core leadership skills:**

- **Planning & workface planning** — set the crews up to succeed: right materials, tools, info, and access ready before they start.
- **Communication** — clear direction up (to the PM/owner), down (to crews), and across (to trades). Most field problems are communication problems.
- **Setting expectations and accountability** — define the standard for safety, quality, and productivity, and hold people to it fairly.
- **Handling conflict and tough conversations** — directly, respectfully, and early.
- **Developing people** — mentor apprentices and younger field staff; the industry runs on it.

**How to lead well in the field:**

1. **Plan the day/week before it starts** — the look-ahead and Weekly Work Plan (Ch. 3) are your leadership tools.
2. **Communicate the plan** clearly at daily huddles — what, where, who, and any hazards.
3. **Be visible and walk the work** — lead from the field, not the trailer.
4. **Address issues early and directly** — a small correction now prevents a big problem later.
5. **Recognize good work** and invest in developing your people.

**Common mistakes:** Leading from the trailer; unclear direction that causes rework; avoiding tough conversations until they blow up; not developing the next generation of field leaders.
