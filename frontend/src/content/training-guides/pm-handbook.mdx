# Alleato Group — Project Manager / Project Engineer Handbook

*Your partner from the ground up.*

Each chapter is a practical, step-by-step "how we do it" guide. Pair each chapter with the curated videos and courses in the Training Hub to go deeper.

## 1. Reading & Reviewing Construction Drawings

**Why it matters:** The drawings are the single source of truth for what gets built. If you can't navigate a set fast and spot conflicts, everything downstream — submittals, buyout, RFIs — suffers.

**Know the set organization.** A commercial set is ordered by discipline, each with a letter prefix:

- **G** — General (cover, code, life safety, symbols legend)
- **C** — Civil (site, grading, utilities)
- **A** — Architectural (plans, elevations, sections, details, schedules)
- **S** — Structural (foundations, framing, details)
- **M / P / E / FP** — Mechanical, Plumbing, Electrical, Fire Protection
- **FA / T / LS** — Fire Alarm, Technology/low-voltage, Life Safety

Sheet numbers read discipline → type → sequence (e.g., **A-201** = Architectural, elevations, sheet 1).

**How to review a new set:**

1. Read the **cover sheet and general notes** first — project scope, code data, sheet index, abbreviations, and symbols legend.
2. Walk the set **front to back once** to build a mental map before diving into details.
3. **Cross-reference disciplines.** Overlay architectural, structural, and MEP for the same area. Look for clashes: a beam where a duct needs to run, a wall that lands on nothing, doors that swing into equipment.
4. Check that **details actually exist.** Follow every detail callout bubble (e.g., "5/A-501") and confirm the referenced detail is present and matches.
5. **Compare drawings against specs.** The drawings show *where/how much*; the specs say *what quality/product*. They must agree.
6. Log every gap, conflict, or ambiguity — that list becomes your first round of RFIs (Chapter 4).

**Common mistakes:** Trusting one discipline without coordinating the others; missing revision clouds/deltas on reissued sheets; assuming a detail applies everywhere it's referenced.

**Alleato tip:** Use Bluebeam to overlay disciplines and to keep a running markup set. Always confirm you're working from the **current** revision — check the revision block and any addenda/ASIs.

## 2. Reading & Understanding Specifications

**Why it matters:** Specs define the quality standard, approved manufacturers, submittal requirements, and execution methods. Buyout, submittals, and QC all trace back to the specs.

**Know the structure — CSI MasterFormat.** Specs are organized into numbered Divisions (00–48). A few you'll live in:

- **Div 00 / 01** — Procurement & Contracting Requirements / General Requirements (the "rules of the job": submittals, closeout, temporary facilities, quality). **Read Division 01 in full — it governs everything.**
- **Div 03** Concrete, **Div 04** Masonry, **Div 05** Metals, **Div 07** Thermal/Moisture, **Div 08** Openings, **Div 09** Finishes
- **Div 21–23** Fire Suppression / Plumbing / HVAC, **Div 26** Electrical

Each section has three parts: **Part 1 General** (submittals, quality, warranties), **Part 2 Products** (materials, approved manufacturers), **Part 3 Execution** (installation).

**How to use specs:**

1. Read **Division 01** cover to cover — it sets submittal procedures, schedule requirements, and closeout obligations.
2. For each trade, pull the relevant section and highlight: required submittals, approved/named manufacturers, "or equal" language, and any performance requirements.
3. Feed **Part 1 submittal lists** directly into your submittal log (Chapter 3).
4. Note warranty durations for the closeout package (Chapter 12).

**Alleato tip:** For ASRS and fire-protection scopes, the specs frequently reference FM Global and NFPA standards — read those requirements carefully during buyout; they drive both product selection and inspection.

## 3. Submittal Review & Management

**Why it matters:** Submittals are how you confirm what the sub/vendor intends to furnish actually matches the contract documents — *before* it's fabricated and delivered. Blowing the submittal schedule is one of the top causes of procurement delays.

**Types of submittals:** shop drawings, product data / cut sheets, samples, mock-ups, test reports, certifications, and (at the end) O&M data and warranties.

**The submittal workflow:**

1. **Build the submittal log** from Division 01 and each spec section's Part 1. List every required submittal, spec section, responsible sub, and required-on-site date working backward from the schedule.
2. **Sub prepares and submits.** They stamp/review it first — never accept an unreviewed submittal from a subcontractor.
3. **PE first-pass review.** Check it against the spec and drawings: correct product, correct quantities/dimensions, meets performance and code requirements, all specified data included. Flag deviations.
4. **Route to the Architect/Engineer (A/E)** for their action.
5. **Log the returned action:** *Approved / Approved as Noted / Revise & Resubmit / Rejected.* Only "Approved" or "Approved as Noted" releases the item for fabrication/order.
6. **Release procurement** once approved — this is the handoff to the procurement log (Chapter 5).
7. **Track resubmittals** to closure.

**Reviewing a submittal — the checklist:**

- Does it match the **spec section** (product, model, finish, performance)?
- Does it match the **drawings** (dimensions, quantities, locations)?
- Are **all specified components/data** included?
- Any **substitutions or "or-equal"** requests flagged and justified?
- Does it satisfy **code / FM / NFPA** requirements where applicable?
- Is the **lead time** compatible with the schedule? (If not, that's a red flag *now*.)

**Common mistakes:** Rubber-stamping a sub's submittal without checking it against the spec; not tracking lead times so a "coordination" issue becomes a "we can't get it in time" crisis; losing track of resubmittals.

**Alleato tip:** Manage the log in our project management software. Sort by required-on-site date, not submission date — that's what protects the schedule.

## 4. RFIs — Requests for Information

**Why it matters:** RFIs resolve gaps, conflicts, and ambiguities in the documents in writing, creating a clear record. A well-written RFI gets a fast, usable answer; a vague one bounces back and burns days.

**How to write a good RFI:**

1. **State the question clearly and narrowly** — one issue per RFI when possible.
2. **Cite the exact reference** — sheet number, detail, spec section, or grid line.
3. **Explain the conflict or gap** — what you see and why it can't be built as drawn.
4. **Propose a solution.** RFIs that suggest an answer get resolved fastest. ("We propose routing the duct below the beam and furring the ceiling to 9'-0". Please confirm.")
5. **Note schedule/cost impact** if there is one, and any needed-by date.
6. **Attach a marked-up sketch or photo.**

**Managing RFIs:** Log every RFI with a number, date opened, needed-by date, and status. Follow up on anything approaching its needed-by date. When an answer changes scope, cost, or time, it becomes a potential change order (Chapter 7) — connect the two.

**Common mistakes:** Using RFIs to ask questions already answered in the documents; burying multiple unrelated questions in one RFI; not tracking the schedule impact of a slow response.

## 5. Material Procurement & the Procurement Log

**Why it matters:** On commercial jobs, long-lead materials (switchgear, ASRS racking/components, structural steel, mechanical equipment, specialty fire-protection items) drive the schedule. The procurement log is how you make sure everything is ordered in time to arrive when the schedule needs it.

**What a procurement log tracks — per line item:**

- Item / description and **spec section**
- Responsible sub or vendor
- **Submittal status** (must be approved before ordering)
- **Order/release date** and PO number
- **Lead time**
- **Required-on-site date** (from the schedule)
- **Fabrication + ship dates** and delivery status
- Cost code

**How to build and run it:**

1. Start from the submittal log and the schedule. For every material item, work **backward from the required-on-site date**: subtract shipping, subtract fabrication, subtract submittal approval time — that gives you the *must-order-by* date.
2. Identify **long-lead items early** and flag them; these get management attention.
3. Don't order until the submittal is **approved** (Chapter 3).
4. Update the log continuously — approval dates, PO dates, ship confirmations, deliveries.
5. Review it in every OAC and internal project meeting. A slipping order date is an early warning.

**Common mistakes:** Building the log too late; not tying required-on-site dates to the real schedule; treating it as a static spreadsheet instead of a living tracker; forgetting that submittal approval must precede the order.

**Alleato tip:** Long-lead ASRS and fire-protection equipment can carry very long lead times — get these identified and submitted in the first weeks of the job.

## 6. Subcontractor Buyout & Writing Scopes of Work

**Why it matters:** Buyout is where the estimate becomes real subcontracts. The single biggest risk is **scope gaps and overlaps** — work that no sub included, or work two subs both assume the other has. A tight scope of work prevents change orders and finger-pointing later.

**The buyout process:**

1. **Prepare bid packages / Invitations to Bid (ITBs)** by trade, referencing the exact drawings, specs, and addenda.
2. **Solicit and level bids.** "Leveling" means comparing bids apples-to-apples: confirm each sub covered the full scope, note inclusions/exclusions, and identify who missed what.
3. **Confirm scope coverage across all packages.** Lay every trade's scope side by side and check that the whole project is covered once — no gaps, no double-coverage.
4. **Negotiate and select.** Consider price, schedule, capacity, safety record, and past performance — not price alone.
5. **Write the subcontract and scope of work** (below).
6. **Issue the subcontract** with the correct exhibits (drawings/spec list, schedule, insurance requirements, safety requirements).

**Writing a subcontract scope of work — how to make it airtight:**

- **Reference the contract documents** explicitly: "Furnish all labor, material, equipment, and supervision to complete the [trade] work per drawings [list] and specifications Section(s) [list], including addenda [list]."
- **Spell out inclusions** — the specific tasks, and any items that commonly fall through the cracks (layout, hoisting, temp protection, cleanup of own debris, as-builts, permits/fees, testing, warranty).
- **Spell out exclusions** — so there's no ambiguity about what was priced.
- **Assign the gray-area items** clearly: who provides blocking/backing, who provides access panels, who cores/patches, who provides fire-stopping, who does final cleaning.
- **Reference the schedule** and the sub's manpower/coordination obligations.
- **State submittal, closeout, and warranty obligations.**
- Use **clear, active language** and consistent terminology tied to the CSI section.

**Common mistakes:** Copy-pasting a generic scope without tailoring it to the actual documents; leaving gray-area items unassigned; not listing exclusions; forgetting to reference addenda/ASIs so the sub prices an outdated set.

**Alleato tip:** Keep a standard "commonly missed items" checklist and run every scope against it before issuing.

## 7. Change Orders & Change Management

**Why it matters:** Changes are inevitable. Managing them cleanly protects the budget, the schedule, and the relationship with the owner. Uncontrolled changes ("just do it, we'll figure it out later") destroy margin.

**The golden rule:** *Never perform changed work without written authorization.* Get direction in writing (a signed change order or, at minimum, a written change directive) before proceeding.

**The change order process:**

1. **Identify the change** — from an RFI answer, an owner request, a field condition, or a design revision (ASI/bulletin).
2. **Notify** per the contract's notice requirements and timeframe — miss the notice window and you can forfeit the right to recover.
3. **Price it** — get sub pricing, add self-perform costs, general conditions, and markup per the contract.
4. **Assess schedule impact** — added days, and request a time extension if warranted.
5. **Submit the change order** with backup.
6. **Get it signed before doing the work** whenever possible. If the owner needs work to proceed before pricing is settled, use a written **Construction Change Directive** to document authorization.
7. **Log it** and roll approved COs into the updated contract value, schedule of values, and budget.

**Common mistakes:** Doing the work first and pricing later; missing contractual notice deadlines; forgetting the schedule/time-extension impact; not updating the SOV and budget after approval.

## 8. Pay Applications & Progress Billing

**Why it matters:** The pay app is how the project gets paid. Accuracy and timeliness protect cash flow. The industry standard is the **AIA G702/G703** pair.

**Know the forms:**

- **G702 (Application and Certificate for Payment)** — the cover sheet: contract sum, change orders, work completed to date, retainage, previous payments, and current amount due.
- **G703 (Continuation Sheet)** — the line-by-line breakdown, driven by the **Schedule of Values (SOV)**.

**The Schedule of Values** divides the contract sum into line items (by trade/CSI or work area). It must total the contract value and be structured so progress can be measured fairly each month.

**How to bill:**

1. Establish the **SOV** early and get it approved.
2. Each period, determine **percent complete** for each line item (with the field/super's input) and the value of **stored materials** if allowed.
3. Fill the **G703** — this period, previous periods, total to date, balance to finish, retainage.
4. Roll totals to the **G702**, subtract retainage and prior payments → current amount due.
5. Collect **sub pay apps and lien waivers**, submit by the contract deadline, and reconcile against your SOV.

**Common mistakes:** Over- or under-billing a line item (front-loading invites owner pushback); forgetting retainage; missing the monthly cutoff; SOV that doesn't let you bill the way work actually progresses.

## 9. Project Scheduling

**Why it matters:** The schedule is the plan for *how* the project gets built in time. PMs must be able to read a CPM schedule, understand the critical path, and drive short-interval planning.

**Key concepts:**

- **CPM (Critical Path Method)** — activities linked by logic; the **critical path** is the longest chain that determines the finish date. Delay a critical activity and you delay the job.
- **Float** — how much an activity can slip without delaying the finish.
- **Baseline vs. updates** — the baseline is the approved plan; updates track actual progress against it.
- **Look-ahead (short-interval) schedule** — the 3–6 week detailed plan used to run the work week to week (see the field track for pull planning / Last Planner).
- Tools: **Primavera P6** (large/complex jobs) and **MS Project** (common on smaller ones).

**What a PM does with the schedule:**

1. Develop or review the **baseline** with the team and subs; validate durations and logic.
2. Tie **procurement and submittals** to schedule dates (Chapters 3 & 5).
3. Run **weekly look-aheads** and update actual progress.
4. Watch the **critical path** and float; when it's threatened, plan recovery (resequence, add crews/shifts).
5. Use schedule data to support **time-extension requests** in change orders.

**Common mistakes:** Treating the baseline as a document that gets filed instead of a tool that gets updated; ignoring float until the path is already blown; not linking procurement to the schedule.

## 10. Cost Control & Job Cost Management

**Why it matters:** The budget is only as good as your ability to track actual costs against it and forecast the outcome. Cost control is how you protect margin and give leadership an honest picture.

**Core concepts:**

- **Budget / cost codes** — the estimate broken into trackable buckets.
- **Committed cost** — subcontracts and POs you've signed.
- **Actual cost** — what's been spent to date.
- **Cost to complete / forecast** — your projection of the final cost per code.
- **Projected margin** — contract value (with approved COs) minus forecast cost.

**The monthly cost cycle:**

1. Update **committed costs** as subcontracts and POs are issued.
2. Capture **actuals** (labor, invoices, self-perform).
3. Review each cost code: over/under budget, and **why**.
4. Update the **cost-to-complete forecast** — this, not just spend-to-date, is what predicts the outcome.
5. Report variances and the projected final margin.
6. Tie changes into the budget (Chapter 7).

**Common mistakes:** Watching spend-to-date without forecasting cost-to-complete; not booking committed costs so exposure is hidden; ignoring small overruns until they compound.

## 11. Construction Contracts

**Why it matters:** The contract defines the deal — scope, price, schedule, risk, and the procedures you must follow (notice, changes, payment, disputes). Knowing "what the contract says" turns arguments into references.

**The two standard families:**

- **AIA Contract Documents** — e.g., **A101** (Owner–Contractor agreement), **A201** (General Conditions — the operational rulebook), **B101** (Owner–Architect). Widely used; architect-administered.
- **ConsensusDocs** — the collaborative-industry alternative, generally viewed as more balanced across parties.

**Common delivery / pricing structures:** Lump Sum (Stipulated Sum), Cost-Plus (with or without GMP), and Design-Build (Alleato's model — one entity carries design and construction risk).

**What a PM must know from the contract:**

- **Scope and exclusions** — what you're obligated to deliver.
- **Change procedures and notice deadlines** (Chapter 7).
- **Payment terms, retainage, and billing schedule** (Chapter 8).
- **Schedule obligations and liquidated damages** (LDs) for late completion.
- **Insurance, bonding, and indemnification** requirements.
- **Dispute resolution** process.

**Alleato tip:** As a design-build firm, Alleato carries design responsibility — pay special attention to how design scope, standard of care, and owner-furnished information are handled.

**Note:** This chapter is general guidance, not legal advice. Loop in leadership/counsel on contract questions with real risk.

## 12. Project Closeout

**Why it matters:** A clean closeout gets you paid (including retainage), releases the team, and protects Alleato's reputation and warranty position. Closeout done as an afterthought drags on for months.

**The closeout package typically includes:**

- **Punch list** complete and signed off
- **As-built / record drawings**
- **O&M manuals** (operation & maintenance data)
- **Warranties and guarantees** (with start dates)
- **Attic stock / spare parts** per specs
- **Final inspections and Certificate of Occupancy**
- **Training** for owner's operations staff (where required)
- **Final lien waivers** and final change order reconciliation
- **Consent of Surety** and final pay application

**How to run it:**

1. **Start early** — build the closeout requirements from Division 01 during preconstruction, not at the end.
2. Collect **O&M, warranty, and as-built data as it becomes available**, not in a final scramble.
3. Manage the **punch list** systematically — walk with the A/E and owner, assign items to subs, track to zero.
4. Coordinate **final inspections and C of O** with the AHJ.
5. Deliver the **owner training** and turnover package.
6. Submit the **final pay app** and release retainage.

**Common mistakes:** Treating closeout as a post-completion task; letting subs demobilize before punch and turnover docs are done; missing warranty start dates; incomplete as-builts.
